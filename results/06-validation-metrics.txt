Threshold-free metrics on validation
====================================

Every metric this project has quoted so far sits at one operating point: the
0.15 malignant threshold, or the top class. None of them answer "how good is
the ranking itself", which is the question that survives changing the
threshold. These two do.

Validation, 1521 images, 293 of them malignant (19.3%). The test set is not
touched here and still stands at the single measurement in results/05.

Validation is not impartial and these numbers inherit that. Not through the
threshold, which is what makes them worth having, but through the weights:
both models were saved at their best validation loss, so validation picked the
epoch. Read them as an upper bound, not a forecast.

Model
-----
Both models exactly as they stand in weights/, no retraining. ResNet18
fine-tuned for 15 epochs, best epoch 7, weights/resnet.pth. The from-scratch
CNN at 40 epochs, weights/cnn.pth. Same normalisation each was trained with,
no augmentation at inference.

    uv run python -m src.evaluate resnet
    uv run python -m src.evaluate cnn

Threshold-free metrics for the malignant call
---------------------------------------------
The score being ranked is the sum of the three malignant probabilities, which
is the same number predict.py compares against MALIGNANT_THRESHOLD. So this
measures the decision the app makes, not a neighbouring one.

                        ResNet18       CNN     gap
    ROC-AUC                0.910     0.868   0.042
    PR-AUC                 0.721     0.544   0.177

For context, at the operating points already recorded:

                        ResNet18       CNN
    accuracy               78.2%     62.2%
    recall, top class      77.5%     77.8%
    precision, top class   58.8%     44.7%
    malignant missed          66        65

A model answering nv every time scores 67.2% accuracy on this split. A model
ranking at random scores PR-AUC 0.193, the share of malignant images, and
ROC-AUC 0.500.

What the numbers say
--------------------
ResNet18 ranks lesions well. PR-AUC 0.721 against a 0.193 floor means the
malignant score carries real information, and it is the first number in this
project that says so without resting on a threshold I chose by hand.

The interesting part is the two gaps. On ROC-AUC the two models look almost
alike, 0.910 against 0.868, a difference of 0.042. On PR-AUC the gap is 0.177,
four times wider. The same two models, the same predictions, two measures that
disagree about how different they are.

ROC-AUC is the one that misleads here. It counts true negatives, and with four
benign lesions for every malignant one, correctly ignoring moles is most of
what either model does. Both are good at it, so both score well and the
difference between them gets diluted. PR-AUC never looks at a true negative.
It asks only how cleanly the 293 malignant lesions were pulled to the top, and
on that the CNN is much worse. 0.177 is the honest size of the gap.

This also settles something the top-class numbers made confusing. The CNN's
recall, 77.8%, is slightly better than ResNet's 77.5%, and it misses one fewer
malignant lesion, 65 against 66. Read on its own, that looks like a tie. It is
not: the CNN raises 282 false alarms to ResNet's 159 to get there, and PR-AUC
prices that in. Two models can miss the same number of cancers and still be
far apart, and this is the measure that shows it.

Nothing here changes any previously recorded number. These are new metrics on
unchanged weights.

Confidence intervals on the malignant call (2026-11-03)
-------------------------------------------------------
Recall and precision on the malignant call share a numerator, and their
denominators move together as the sample changes: one more malignant
lesion grows recall's denominator, one more false alarm grows precision's.
A Wilson interval looks at one ratio at a time and cannot see that, so
these two were bootstrapped instead: redraw 1521 images with replacement,
recompute both, 10000 times, keep the middle 95%. Seeded, so the command
prints the same range every time.

Validation, top-class rule:

    metric       value          bootstrap            Wilson     width
    recall      77.5%   72.6% - 82.1%   72.4% - 81.9%    9.5pt
    precision   58.8%   53.9% - 63.7%   53.8% - 63.6%    9.8pt

What the numbers say
--------------------
Recall is 77.5% and could reasonably have been anywhere from 72.6% to
82.1%. Precision is 58.8% against a range of 53.9% to 63.7%. Both are
about ten points wide, which is the honest resolution of a 1521-image
split, and worth knowing before quoting either to one decimal place.

The two methods agree, and that is not what I expected. I went into this
assuming the bootstrap would come out wider for precision, because it
sees the denominator move and Wilson does not. It does not: the bounds
differ by at most 0.2 points and the widths match to one decimal.

The reason is sample size. At 293 malignant lesions and 386 malignant
calls, the denominators barely wobble compared with the numerators, so
the effect Wilson misses is too small to show up. The shared numerator
matters for how the two metrics move together, not for how wide either
one is on its own.

So the bootstrap did not buy a different number here. It bought a check:
two unrelated methods on the same data landing in the same place is
evidence that neither is wrong. For these two metrics, at this sample
size, Wilson would have been enough. That stops being true for a
statistic with no closed form, which is where the bootstrap earns its
keep, and it is why the function stays.

Nothing measured changes. These are ranges around figures already
recorded in this file.
